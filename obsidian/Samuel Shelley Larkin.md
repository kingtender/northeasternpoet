# Samuel · Shelley · Larkin

Larkin's "The Whitsun Weddings" set beside *The Masque of Anarchy*.

Related: [[Peterloo and The Mask of Anarchy]] · [[Anabelle Shelley Adorno]] · [[The Mask of Anarchy — Student Handout]]

**Texts.** Philip Larkin, "The Whitsun Weddings," in *The Whitsun Weddings* (Faber, 1964). Eight ten-line stanzas, rhyming ababcdecde, a Shakespearean quatrain followed by a Petrarchan sestet, the pattern often compared to Keats's odes. A southbound train out of Paragon station, Hull, toward London. Shelley, *The Mask of Anarchy*, composed autumn 1819, published 1832. The "Masque" spelling follows Hunt's first edition and is defensible.

---

## Sequence for the response

Tell him the comparison is stronger than he knows, give him the fact that makes it hold, then hand him the objection he has to answer. In that order.

---

## 1. The fact that makes it real

Whitsun is not an arbitrary English holiday. In industrial Lancashire, Whit week was the **wakes week**, when the cotton mills closed and the whole town stopped. The **Manchester Whit Walks began in 1801**, out of the Sunday-school movement, organised so that mill children would have something to do on their day off. Walkers processed in their best clothes, behind banners, with bands, in competitive display of dress.

Eighteen years later the contingents who came into St Peter's Field came in that idiom: Sunday best, banners, bands, drilled on the moors so they could walk in good order, women's societies in white. The magistrates read the drilling as military preparation. The walkers had learned the form at Whitsun.

So Larkin, titling a poem about English collective ritual in motion "The Whitsun Weddings," has named, without meaning anything by it, the calendar and the processional repertoire out of which Peterloo was assembled. Not a resemblance a student invented. A real structure in the social history of the same region.

> [!note] Status
> The underlying facts are solid. The connection between the Whit Walk repertoire and the Peterloo processions is **my inference**. Send him to Katrina Navickas, *Protest and the Politics of Space and Place, 1789–1848* (Manchester University Press, 2016), which covers processions, the occupation of public space, and industrialising Lancashire and Yorkshire, to find out whether it has been made properly and by whom.

## 2. The formal link that carries weight

Both poems end pointing at a force that has been gathered and is about to be released, and both decline to name its object.

Shelley:
> 'Rise like Lions after slumber
> In unvanquishable number,

Larkin: the couples' shared future "Stood ready to be loosed with all the power / That being changed can give," then "there swelled / A sense of falling, like an arrow-shower / Sent out of sight, somewhere becoming rain."

**An arrow-shower is a weapon, and Larkin's simile lands it as weather.** Put him on that one word. A poem that turns massed arrows into rain is doing something to the possibility of collective force, and whatever it is doing is the opposite of what Shelley does in the same figure.

## 3. The objection he must answer

Larkin's speaker is structurally a non-participant. The architecture is a man at a window who notices the weddings late, watches from a compartment he never leaves, and is tender toward the couples in a register inseparable from condescension: "The nylon gloves and jewellery-substitutes."

Shelley's poem issues instructions. Where to assemble, how to stand, what to do when the horsemen come.

Different speech acts. A comparison treating both as poems about English collective life has flattened the only interesting thing in the pairing. Make him name the difference in address before he keeps the resemblance. The Keatsian ten-line stanza is itself an argument about who is listening, since nobody chants that, while the tetrameter of the *Masque* was built to be shouted by people who did not own the book.

## 4. The turn that will teach him something

Run the pair through Adorno's criterion (see [[Anabelle Shelley Adorno]]) and **Larkin wins**. Adorno holds that the lyric reaches the collective only by refusing to assert it, and that poems announcing a we produce ideology. Larkin never says we. The poem descends into one man's afternoon and the social arrives unbidden, in the gloves and the substitutes and the arrow-shower. Shelley says "Ye are many—they are few," precisely the move Adorno distrusts.

Make him sit in that result rather than escape it. Either the criterion is right and the agitational poem is the aesthetically weaker one, or the criterion cannot see a poem that lived by being recited to thousands, which is a real limit he can prove with the *Northern Star*.

## 5. What to tell him not to do

Do not let it become a thesis about English collectivity across the centuries. The comparison earns its keep as a controlled contrast of address, occasion, and what each poem does with latent force, anchored in one concrete link that is historical rather than thematic.

Two poems, one holiday, one procession, opposite verdicts on what a crowd is.

If he takes the Whitsun line seriously he has a paper. If he wanted only a resemblance he has a paragraph.
