# Siqi · Shelley

Response to the paper on nonviolence, affect, and how much faith to have in poetry.

Related: [[Peterloo and The Mask of Anarchy]] · [[Nell Shelley Casanova]] · [[Sean Shelley reception history]] · [[Anabelle Shelley Adorno]] · [[Samuel Shelley Larkin]]

**Verdict.** The most philosophically ambitious of the three papers, and it rests on a misattribution which, once corrected, hands her a far better argument than the one she wrote. Her closing question is answered by the poem's pronoun system, and the answer is not the one she fears.

---

## 1. The correction that reorganises everything

**The speech is not Hope's.** Hope speaks twelve lines, sts. 23–24, and they are pure lament:

> 'My father Time is weak and gray
> With waiting for a better day;
> See how idiot-like he stands,
> Fumbling with his palsied hands!

ending "Misery, oh, Misery!" That is all she says. Then she lies down in the road.

The long speech from st. 37 is attributed, at sts. 34–36, to **the Earth**:

> As if their own indignant Earth
> Which gave the sons of England birth
> Had felt their blood upon her brow,
> And shuddering with a mother's throe
>
> Had turned every drop of blood
> By which her face had been bedewed
> To an accent unwithstood,—
> As if her heart had cried aloud:

So her wry aside is exactly inverted. **Hope performs the poem's only physical act and says almost nothing; the doctrine of nonviolence belongs to the ground.**

## 2. It answers her central question

She asks whether nonviolent protest and poetry both work on affect, and so whether faith in one is faith in the other. The poem's answer is more materialist than she expects. The Earth "had turned every drop of blood ... To an accent unwithstood." **The speech is made out of the blood.** Not inspired by it. Converted from it, drop by drop, by ground that has absorbed it. And the result is *unwithstood*, irresistible rather than persuasive.

That is a theory in which atrocity manufactures its own language and the language needs nobody's faith in order to work. Which reframes her question: the poem is not asking how much faith to place in affect; it claims that massacre is self-transcribing, leaving the poet only transcription. Set that beside [[Nell Shelley Casanova|Nell's]] finding that the narrating I vanishes after st. 9 and never returns, and the two lock: **the poem removes the poet because on its own theory the poet is not needed.**

> [!note] Dew, three times
> "A shower of crimson dew" (st. 29) · "By which her face had been bedewed" (st. 36) · "Shake your chains to earth like dew" (st. 38). Blood, transcription, liberation. The middle instance is the hinge. This belongs in all three marking notes.

## 3. "Solely on affect" is too narrow, and the correction has teeth

Three stanzas against that claim:

**St. 83, liability.** "On those who first should violate / Such sacred heralds in their state / Rest the blood that must ensue, / And it will not rest on you." A legal-moral claim, not a feeling.

**St. 87, local sanction.** "Every woman in the land / Will point at them as they stand— / They will hardly dare to greet / Their acquaintance in the street." Precisely calibrated to Peterloo: the Manchester and Salford Yeomanry were **local men**, manufacturers and publicans and shopkeepers, who had to go on living among the people they cut and who would in fact meet them in the street. Shelley worked this out in Italy from newspapers.

**St. 88, defection.** "And the bold, true warriors / Who have hugged Danger in wars / Will turn to those who would be free, / Ashamed of such base company." The professionals abandoning the amateurs: a strategic claim about splitting the coercive apparatus.

So shame is the medium and the mechanism is **fracture**. Harder-headed than faith in feeling, and it makes the paper an argument rather than a meditation.

## 4. Her "Some" is right and can be evidenced

- Mechanism one **failed**: *Redford v. Birley* went to the defendants at Lancaster, 4–9 April 1822. No liability landed.
- Mechanism two **held**: Birley remained locally notorious for the rest of his life.
- Mechanism three is **arguable**: the yeomanry option was politically discredited, and the Combination Laws were repealed in June 1824, without a vote and without debate.

One prediction wrong, one right, one plausible on a timescale of years rather than the weeks the poem seems to imagine. That is what "some" looks like documented.

## 5. Push back on one sentence

> "That willingness to be attacked and the absence of rage vis-à-vis injustice is, doubtlessly, opposite to human nature."

Weakest sentence in the paper; should not survive revision. An unargued universal, contradicted in the poem's immediate vicinity. The Peterloo contingents had **drilled on the moors** in order to process in disciplined order; the magistrates read the drilling as military preparation, the radicals meant it as training in restraint. Two years earlier the Blanketeers assembled on the same ground and were dispersed by cavalry without fighting back. Lancashire had a processional tradition going back to the Manchester Whit Walks of 1801 (see [[Samuel Shelley Larkin]]).

Standing still under attack is a **trained, institutionally transmitted disposition**, not a violation of nature. Which converts her sadness into a better question: what institutions train people to do this, what does the training cost the trained, and who benefits.

## 6. Small fixes

- **Tiananmen or Tahrir.** She writes Tiananmen; the connection that circulates is Tahrir 2011. I cannot find the Tiananmen claim either way. Needs a source or needs to go.
- **Drop the Wikipedia citation.** Hunt's "flaming robe of verse" is from his own preface to the 1832 edition; Mary Shelley quotes it in her 1839 note. For reception, cite Michael Demson, "'Let a great Assembly be': Percy Shelley's 'The Mask of Anarchy' and the Organization of Labor in New York City, 1910–30," *European Romantic Review* 22:5 (2011), 641–665.
- **"Prosecutor" for "persecutor"** in the final sentence, where she had it right earlier.

## 7. The last line, and the pronoun system that answers it

> "Should I then willingly lie under the prosecutor's gun when I am not sure all this will happen? Maybe?"

Right ending, and I would not let her fix it. But the poem answers it, in grammar.

### The counts

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **thou / thee / thy / thine** | 27 | Deities only. Anarchy, addressed by the murderers and lawyers ("Thou art God, and Law, and King"), and Liberty, addressed by the Earth through the litany of sts. 52–65: "What art thou Freedom?", "Thou art Justice", "Thou art Wisdom", "Thou art Peace", "Thou art Love". |
| **ye / your** | 28 | The people, without exception. |
| **we / us / our** | 7 | **Five belong to the counter-revolutionary bloc.** St. 16, the hired murderers: "We have waited, weak and lone / For thy coming, Mighty One! / Our purses are empty, our swords are cold, / Give us glory, and blood, and gold." St. 18, lawyers and priests: "Anarchy, to thee we bow." One is the narrator's sarcasm (st. 20, "our Kings"). One only might include the people (st. 55, "As in England now we see"), and even that is a seeing, not a doing. |

> [!important] The only "we" in the poem belongs to power
> The oppressors have a first person plural and the people do not. The people are addressed in the second person by a speaker who is the ground they stand on, and they never once speak as a collective subject. The poem **convenes a "ye" and withholds a "we"**, so the solidarity it calls for is grammatically marked as not yet having happened.

### The one singular

The poem addresses a person in the singular exactly once. St. 49: birds find nests, beasts find lairs. St. 50:

> 'Asses, swine, have litter spread
> And with fitting food are fed;
> All things have a home but one—
> Thou, Oh, Englishman, hast none!

St. 51 opens: "This is Slavery."

**The singular is the grammatical form of destitution.** To be "thou" in this poem, if you are a person rather than a god, is to be the one thing in creation without a home. Eleven stanzas earlier: "Do not thus when ye are strong." Eighteen earlier: "Ye are many." The plural is the form of having something.

So the poem does have a place for her "I." It is st. 50, and occupying it is what the poem calls slavery. Her question is not one the text declines to answer; it is the symptom the text is describing. A reader who arrives at "should I" has been correctly identified as someone without an assembly, and the sensation of being asked for solitary martyrdom is the poem's **diagnosis** rather than its demand.

Tell her this and the sadness she reports becomes evidence rather than mood.

### Why *ye* and not *you*

By 1819 "you" had absorbed both numbers, so "ye" is the specific archaism that restores a plural English had lost. Shelley chose a pronoun that **cannot be singular**. "Ye are many" is tautological at the level of grammar, which is the redundancy of ritual rather than a slip, and the archaism simultaneously imports the King James register the blood-into-speech mechanism needs to sound prophetic.

Which makes the individualising slip structural. The poem's whole defence against being heard as addressed to one person rests on an archaic pronoun, and archaism erodes first. A reader in 2017 hears "ye" as antique flavouring rather than as a prohibition. **The protection decays while the line survives**, which is a precise mechanism for how a poem addressed to a massed assembly becomes available to a festival crowd told that "those" people will be enriched and "the rest of us" made secure.

### Two consequences

**Corbyn's "those" is the soldiers' pronoun.** The poem's single third-person reference to the oppressed is st. 88, where the regulars "Will turn to those who would be free." That is how the army will see the crowd at the moment of its conversion; the poem's own address never uses it. See [[Sean Shelley reception history]].

**This is why the refrain travels and the instructions do not.** "Ye are many—they are few" is the one line *about* the plural rather than merely addressed to it, so it states its own condition of use and survives extraction. "Stand ye calm and resolute" cannot survive extraction, because a solitary reader receives it only as a demand to be killed alone. Nobody quotes it; everyone quotes the other. Not taste. A consequence of which lines remain sayable once the assembly is gone.

## Open, for seminar

Adorno's charge is against poems that assert a we. This poem gives the we to the enemy and addresses the people as a you it hopes to constitute. **Convening is not claiming.** If that distinction is substantive rather than technical, the *Masque* escapes the objection that appeared to condemn it, and escapes on grammatical grounds. Put it to [[Anabelle Shelley Adorno|Anabelle]].

And [[Samuel Shelley Larkin|Samuel's]] Angelou reading sits in the same opening. Calling "Still I Rise" a dilution of the exhortation assumes the plural was always available. If the "ye" depends on an assembly that can actually be convened, a poem written where none exists may have to relocate the rising in a single body, making the individuation a report on conditions rather than a falling-off. Do not settle this one.
