# Nell · Shelley · Casanova

Response to the paper on voice, exile, and the political purview of language in the *Masque of Anarchy*.

Related: [[Peterloo and The Mask of Anarchy]] · [[Sean Shelley reception history]] · [[Anabelle Shelley Adorno]] · [[The Mask of Anarchy — Student Handout]]

**Verdict.** Genuine formal thinking, working inward from voice where [[Sean Shelley reception history|Sean]] works outward from reception. Two of her three observations are better than she knows. One paragraph needs pushing off a conventional reading. Her theory question has a documented answer in Shelley's own archive.

---

## 1. Lead with the fact that breaks her central claim and improves it

She writes that the poet "remains 'outside of' the public, commentating from the position of a lyric 'I' who is in Italy having a dream — an ultimately interior psychic experience."

> [!important] Track what happens to that I
> It speaks in st. 1, reports the procession, says "On his brow this mark I saw" at st. 9, and then **vanishes**. It never returns. The poem ends at st. 91 inside the Earth's speech, closing the single quotation mark opened at st. 37 and closing nothing else. No waking, no return to Italy, no frame.

So the poem does not sustain the exiled interiority she attributes to it. It liquidates the observing subject partway through and hands the remainder to a voice that is not his. Her observation about voice attribution, which is the best thing in the paper, is more radical than she realised: the exhortation is ventriloquised, and then the ventriloquist is dropped.

**Ask her:** what does it mean that a poem about collective action opens by establishing a private dreaming subject and then declines to give him back?

Related precision. "They are dying whilst I speak" (st. 42), which she reads well as pitting material need against the futility of description, is spoken by **the Shape**, not the narrator. The only moment of anxiety about speech-versus-action in the poem belongs to the borrowed voice. The exiled poet never worries about it. That sharpens her point rather than weakening it.

## 2. Push her off the sublime paragraph

Weakest and most conventional thing here. "To fight for one's freedom is, emotionally, to transcend one's local context and experience the sublime" flattens exactly what is strange in the stanzas she quotes.

Stanza 29, on the Shape's plumes:

> On its helm, seen far away,
> A planet, like the Morning's, lay;
> And those plumes its light rained through
> Like a shower of crimson dew.

**Crimson dew is blood.** Three stanzas later Hope is "ankle-deep in blood" and Anarchy lies "dead earth upon the earth." The nature imagery at the poem's climax is nature saturated with gore. That is contamination of the pastoral, not transcendence through it.

> [!tip] The connection she sets up and misses
> **Dew is the poem's governing figure and it carries both terms.** Crimson dew at st. 29 is blood. "Shake your chains to earth like dew" in the refrain is liberation. She quotes both passages in the same paper without noticing that one word does the work of massacre and of emancipation.
>
> That is a paragraph, and possibly the paper.

## 3. Her Casanova question has an answer, and Shelley gives it

She asks: "when is vernacular plainspokenness a radical or countercultural register, and when does a more esoteric avant-garde aesthetic, distanced from common speech, become apt for challenging the status quo?"

Preface to *Prometheus Unbound*, same year:

> My purpose has hitherto been simply to familiarise the highly refined imagination of the more select classes of poetical readers with beautiful idealisms of moral excellence.

And from the same preface: "Didactic poetry is my abhorrence."

Against Mary Shelley on the *Masque*: "The poem was written for the people, and is therefore in a more popular tone than usual."

**Two explicit, opposite specifications of audience and mode, from one poet, inside twelve months.** Shelley ran both strategies simultaneously. Her question has a documented answer in the archive, and the answer is: both, at once, by the same person, deliberately. See [[Anabelle Shelley Adorno]] §3 for how Casanova's assimilation/differentiation pair makes this legible as strategy rather than inconsistency.

Two cautions on her Casanova use, both worth one sentence in revision:
- Casanova associates exile with the **pursuit of consecration at a literary capital**. Shelley's exile was to Italy, where he sought none, and he published in London.
- England in 1819 was the hegemon. Shelley is not a Casanovan peripheral, so she is borrowing the association without the mechanism. Fine if she says so.

## 4. Smaller notes

**"Could a text that stayed local and specific... constitute poetry?"** Empirical rather than theoretical question. The *Northern Star* poetry columns are full of occasional, local, specific verse: over a thousand poems by more than three hundred and fifty poets. Send her to Mike Sanders, *The Poetry of Chartism* (Cambridge, 2009).

**Qualify the plainspokenness claim.** The poem also contains "targes," "sphereless stars," an "Anarch," the whole allegorical apparatus of a courtly masque, and a rhyme that only works for readers (words/swords, st. 74 — see [[Sean Shelley reception history]] §1). The popular register is adopted unevenly, which is itself the interesting fact.

**Her best close reading, with a textual wrinkle.** She is right and precise that the simile runs the wrong way: Murder "had a mask like Castlereagh" rather than Castlereagh wearing a mask like Murder, so the abstraction is primary and the politician is the vessel du jour. Worth telling her the two manuscripts differ at the Eldon line, the Hunt manuscript reading "Like Eldon" and the Wise manuscript "Like Lord Eldon."

**Her vocalisation catalogue** (moan, groan, wail, "the murmur of distress") is good and under-exploited. If she wants the oral dimension, the archival gift is in Mary Shelley's note: "I heard him repeat, and admired, those beginning 'My Father Time is old and gray,' before I knew to what poem they were to belong." The poem reads "My father Time is **weak** and gray" (st. 23). The transcriber of the manuscript misquotes it in her own note, from memory of hearing it aloud. First circulation oral, first misquotation domestic.
