# *The Mask of Anarchy*
### Shelley, Peterloo, Manchester, 1819

---

## The text you are reading

Shelley composed the poem in the autumn of 1819 at the Villa Valsovano near Livorno, in Italy, within weeks of hearing about Peterloo. He sent the manuscript to Leigh Hunt for publication in *The Examiner*. Hunt did not print it. The poem first appeared in **1832**, thirteen years later, published by Edward Moxon with a preface by Hunt.

**The title is unstable and the instability matters.** Shelley's manuscripts read *Mask*. Hunt's 1832 first edition prints *Masque*. A mask is a disguise, and the poem uses it that way in its second stanza: Murder "had a mask like Castlereagh." A masque is a courtly procession-drama, and the poem uses that too, calling its own parade of figures "this ghastly masquerade." Both readings are in the poem. Your edition has chosen one. Notice which.

The stanza numbers below follow the standard text (Hutchinson, after the Hunt manuscript). Where a quotation ends in a dash or a comma, that is the edition's pointing, not a typo. Stanza 38 and stanza 91 are the same stanza printed twice, with different punctuation the second time. Ask yourself what changes on the repeat.

## Five things to unlearn before you read

1. **There was no riot.** The meeting on 16 August 1819 was legal, announced, rehearsed, and unarmed. People came in their Sunday best with bands and banners.
2. **The regular army did not start it.** The first charge was made by the Manchester and Salford Yeomanry, a part-time local cavalry troop of manufacturers, merchants, publicans and shopkeepers. The 15th Hussars, regulars, came after.
3. **Shelley was not there.** He was in Italy and learned of the massacre from newspapers and letters. The poem says so in its first two lines.
4. **Nobody read this poem in 1819.** Publishing it would have meant a prosecution for seditious libel. It reached print in 1832, in a very different England.
5. **Manchester in 1819 was not yet a city of steam-powered factories.** Most Manchester mills were still water-powered into the early 1820s, and there were more handloom weavers working at home than there were factory operatives. The crowd was largely made up of domestic weavers whose pay was being cut toward starvation.

## What happened at St Peter's Field

Monday, **16 August 1819**. Roughly **60,000** people assembled on open ground in central Manchester to hear the radical orator **Henry Hunt** speak for parliamentary reform. Contingents had marched in from the surrounding towns, having drilled on the moors so that they could process in good order; the magistrates read that drilling as military preparation. Women's reform societies marched in white.

The magistrates issued a warrant for Hunt's arrest and sent in the Yeomanry to serve it. The troop, commanded by **Hugh Hornby Birley**, a Manchester cotton manufacturer, became stuck in the crowd and began cutting with sabres. The Hussars were then ordered in to clear the field.

**At least eighteen people were killed** (accounts give seventeen or eighteen) and roughly **650 to 700 injured**, more than two hundred of them by sabre. Women were disproportionately represented among the wounded.

The Prince Regent thanked the magistrates. Hunt was imprisoned. In December Parliament passed the **Six Acts**, restricting public meetings and taxing the cheap press. In **Redford v. Birley**, a private action for assault tried at Lancaster Assizes from 4 to 9 April 1822, the verdict went to the defendants: the assembly was ruled unlawful and the dispersal justified. No one was ever punished.

**The name is a joke with an argument inside it.** Britain had defeated Napoleon at Waterloo in 1815. The radical *Manchester Observer* called this Peterloo. The army that saved the nation had turned on the nation.

## Manchester in 1819

**Manchester had no Member of Parliament.** It had no municipal corporation either. A town of well over a hundred thousand people was administered by a medieval manorial court leet, a boroughreeve, constables, a body of police commissioners created by act in the early 1790s, and a bench of magistrates answerable to nobody local. The rotten borough of Old Sarum, with almost no inhabitants, returned two members. Manchester returned none until 1832 and was not incorporated until 1838. **The meeting was about representation because there was none.**

Population figures for 1819 are contested because the boundaries were: the township counts around 108,000 in the 1821 census, the "town" is often given as about 126,000, and Manchester together with Salford runs closer to 187,000. There was no single Manchester to count.

**Cotton.** Spinning had been mechanised. Weaving had not. As late as 1813 there were twice as many handloom weavers working in their own homes as there were factory operatives in the whole British cotton industry. Weavers were paid by the piece, and the rates were being driven down year on year. Meanwhile the **Corn Laws** of 1815 kept bread expensive, demobilisation after Waterloo flooded the labour market, and the return to gold in 1819 tightened the currency. Bread dear, work scarce, wages falling.

**One year earlier.** In the summer of 1818 the cotton spinners struck across the district. Pickets went to the mills, among them Birley's. So the man who led the sabre charge in August 1819 had faced this crowd's trade in a wage dispute in the summer of 1818. The industrial conflict and the political one were the same conflict.

## Words that have changed meaning

**Anarchy.** In 1819 this is what loyalists accuse reformers of wanting: chaos from below. **Shelley gives the name to the government.** His Anarchy wears a crown, carries a sceptre, and rides at the head of a procession. Track what the poem does to this word and you have its politics.

**Reform.** Not democracy as we understand it. The demand was votes for all adult men, parliaments elected every year, a secret ballot, and a redistribution of seats to the new industrial towns. These become the points of the People's Charter in 1838, which is how Peterloo connects to Chartism.

**Yeomanry.** Volunteer part-time cavalry, locally raised, self-equipped, socially respectable. Not professional soldiers. Your employer, on a horse, with a sword.

**Seditious libel.** The crime of publishing words tending to bring the government into contempt. The reason this poem waited thirteen years.

**Masque.** A courtly entertainment built around allegorical figures in procession, associated with the Stuart court and with Ben Jonson. Shelley takes the form belonging to power and marches power through it.

## Passages to mark

**Stanza 1.** The poem announces that it is secondhand.
> As I lay asleep in Italy
> There came a voice from over the Sea,
> And with great power it forth led me
> To walk in the visions of Poesy.

*What does it cost the poem to admit this in its first four lines? What does it gain?*

**Stanzas 2 and 8–9.** Real ministers wear allegorical masks; then Anarchy arrives crowned.
> I met Murder on the way—
> He had a mask like Castlereagh—

> Last came Anarchy: he rode
> On a white horse, splashed with blood;
> He was pale even to the lips,
> Like Death in the Apocalypse.

> And he wore a kingly crown;
> And in his grasp a sceptre shone;
> On his brow this mark I saw—
> 'I AM GOD, AND KING, AND LAW!'

*Who is the anarchist in this poem?*

**Stanzas 22 and 32.** Hope enters looking like her opposite, and survives.
> When one fled past, a maniac maid,
> And her name was Hope, she said:
> But she looked more like Despair,

> And the prostrate multitude
> Looked—and ankle-deep in blood,
> Hope, that maiden most serene,
> Was walking with a quiet mien:

**Stanzas 65 and 79–85.** The tactical advice, which is the strangest thing in the poem.
> Let a great Assembly be
> Of the fearless and the free
> On some spot of English ground
> Where the plains stretch wide around.

> 'Stand ye calm and resolute,
> Like a forest close and mute,
> With folded arms and looks which are
> Weapons of unvanquished war,

> 'And if then the tyrants dare
> Let them ride among you there,
> Slash, and stab, and maim, and hew,—
> What they like, that let them do.

> 'With folded arms and steady eyes,
> And little fear, and less surprise,
> Look upon them as they slay
> Till their rage has died away.

*The poem tells an unarmed crowd to stand still and be cut down. Why would that work? Who is the audience for the blood?*

**Stanzas 38 and 91.** The most quoted stanza in English political poetry, printed twice.
> 'Rise like Lions after slumber
> In unvanquishable number,
> Shake your chains to earth like dew
> Which in sleep had fallen on you—
> Ye are many—they are few.

*Hold this against "let them do what they like." Do not resolve the contradiction too quickly.*

## Questions

1. The poem instructs its readers to accept violence without returning it, and it ends by telling them to rise like lions. Are these the same instruction? If they are, what makes them the same?
2. Shelley never saw Manchester. Does the poem's distance from the event weaken it, or is distance part of what it is arguing about, given that its readers would also learn of Peterloo by reading?
3. Compare *The Mask of Anarchy* with Shelley's sonnet "England in 1819," written within weeks of it. One is a ballad you could shout in a street; the other is a single suspended sentence that holds off its main verb for twelve lines. What does each form assume about who is listening?
4. Anarchy in this poem is a king. What happens to a political vocabulary when a poet successfully steals a word from the other side?
5. The poem was written in 1819 and published in 1832, the year Manchester finally got two MPs and working men still got no vote. What does a poem become when it arrives thirteen years late, into the moment of a reform that excluded the people it was written for?

- E. P. Thompson, *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963). For the radical culture the crowd came out of.
