# Anabelle · Shelley · Adorno

Related: [[Peterloo and The Mask of Anarchy]] · [[The Mask of Anarchy — Student Handout]] · [[Samuel Shelley Larkin]]

> [!warning] Verify before quoting
> The account below is paraphrase, worked up from memory and secondary sources rather than from the text in hand. Check all wording against Nicholsen before quoting in print or on a syllabus.

**Text.** Theodor W. Adorno, "On Lyric Poetry and Society." Radio address, 1957. German in *Noten zur Literatur* I (1958). First English by Bruce Mayo in *Telos* 20 (1974). Standard English in *Notes to Literature*, trans. Shierry Weber Nicholsen (Columbia University Press, 1991).

---

## The argument

Adorno opens by conceding the objection: the lyric is the last place to look for society, being the sphere of the private and the withdrawn. He then refuses both obvious ways of joining the terms. Poems are not sociological evidence, and their social content is not their subject matter. Reading a poem for its themes loses the thing that makes it a poem.

The reversal: the lyric's withdrawal from society **is** the social fact. A demand for undisfigured language, for subjectivity untouched by exchange, is legible only against a world in which language and subjectivity have been disfigured by exchange. The poem's refusal to speak of the social registers it more precisely than depiction could. His thesis, in the formulation most often quoted, is that the lyric work is always the subjective expression of a social antagonism. Apparent purity is the negative imprint of what the poem will not say.

**The hinge is language.** The lyric subject achieves expression by surrendering itself to a medium that is objective and collective by nature. So the moment of deepest privacy in a poem is the moment when something other than the individual speaks. The most subjective art works in the most social material, and individuation carried far enough turns into objectivity. Universality is earned by descent into the particular.

**The criterion that matters for us.** Poems taking the collective as their theme, announcing a we and speaking on its behalf, produce ideology. Poems pursuing the individual to its vanishing point arrive at the collective involuntarily, which is the only way it can be arrived at.

**The two readings.** Mörike's "Auf einer Wanderung," where Adorno finds the high classical style and the intimate Biedermeier miniature held in a balance whose fragility is the social content, registering a Germany in which the bourgeois order had not finished arriving. Then a short Stefan George poem beginning "Im windes-weben," where extreme aristocratic esotericism circles back to something like folk song, attaining by artifice what the *Volkslied* had by tradition. *Sources disagree about which George collection that poem belongs to. Leave the collection out until checked.*

His figure for what a poem is, and the phrase for the board: **the philosophical sundial telling the time of history.**

## What it adds to the course

**1. It supplies the best available argument against the poems on the syllabus.** This is the reason to assign it. Most Marxist frames a political poetry course meets agree with the poems, which teaches students only that their teacher approves of Shelley. Adorno's criterion condemns *The Mask of Anarchy*. "Ye are many, they are few" is exactly the assertion of a collective he holds to be the lyric's characteristic failure, and "Men of England, wherefore plough" addresses a we in its first line. Students made to defend Shelley against that have to argue about form, audience, occasion, and what a poem is for.

**Companion text:** "Commitment," in *Notes to Literature* vol. 2, where the case against Brecht and Sartre is explicit. The two essays together are the strongest antagonist the syllabus can field.

**2. It relocates the social from content to form, which fixes the course's chronic failure mode.** Students read political poems as documents and mine them for information about events, the one operation Adorno forbids. Once that route closes, the ballad measure becomes the argument: tetrameter built for shouting, the masque form stolen from the Stuart court and marched through with a crowned Anarchy at its head, the stanza printed twice with different pointing. Adorno makes prosody political without asking anyone to believe meter encodes content.

**3. It historicises the lyric I as an industrial form, which puts him in the same frame as Malm.** Adorno's lyric subject, retreating from a reified world into a language it hopes is clean, is a bourgeois formation with a date, and the date is roughly Malm's. *My inference, past anything Adorno says:* the subject that withdraws from the commodity and the engine that generalises the commodity emerge together, so the pure lyric and fossil capital are contemporaries and possibly co-productive. See [[Malm — Fossil Capital and Manchester]]. This would give the chronology a use beyond correcting omissions.

**4. It handles Shelley's distance.** Students get stuck on an aristocrat in Italy writing for Lancashire weavers and resolve it badly, either debunking him as a tourist or pretending he was one of them. Adorno's account of the subject surrendering to a collective medium licenses a third answer: authority comes from the language rather than the biography, and the gap between writer and addressed is a condition of lyric address rather than a scandal in this case.

## Where the class can beat him

Adorno's lyric has one reader, silent, alone, holding a book. *The Mask of Anarchy* had a movement. Reprinted in the *Northern Star*, recited at meetings, memorised, chanted by people who did not own the book. Mike Sanders, *The Poetry of Chartism* (Cambridge, 2009), documents poetry as collective practice: over a thousand poems by more than three hundred and fifty poets in one newspaper with a mass readership. Adorno's model has no slot for that, and a criterion measuring a poem by depth of individuation cannot see a poem whose whole existence is its circulation. Students holding Sanders against Adorno are doing criticism rather than receiving it, and they can win on the evidence. Give them that week.

**Structural weakness, in the (b) category, an assumption requiring examination.** Locating social content in form means no poem can fail to have social content, and the criterion for success gets applied to poems Adorno already admires. Mörike and George come out well because Mörike and George are the examples. The apparatus has no way of being embarrassed by a poem, which makes it powerful as a reading practice and unreliable as a test.

## The pairing to build the week around

Shelley, *A Defence of Poetry* (written 1821, published 1840):

> The cultivation of those sciences which have enlarged the limits of the empire of man over the external world, has, for want of the poetical faculty, proportionally circumscribed those of the internal world; and man, having enslaved the elements, remains himself a slave. To what but a cultivation of the mechanical arts in a degree disproportioned to the presence of the creative faculty, which is the basis of all knowledge, is to be attributed the abuse of all invention for abridging and combining labour, to the exasperation of the inequality of mankind? From what other cause has it arisen that the discoveries which should have lightened, have added a weight to the curse imposed on Adam?

Adorno, 1957, holds the same diagnosis: mechanised modernity has damaged the faculty that would let us live in it, and poetry is where the damage shows. They split entirely on the remedy. Shelley concludes the poet should write for the many in a meter they can shout. Adorno concludes the poem should refuse the many and become unassimilable.

Same account of the wound, opposite prescriptions, one hundred and thirty-six years apart, both defensible. That is a seminar rather than a lecture, and it does not resolve.
