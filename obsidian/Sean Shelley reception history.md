# Sean · Shelley · reception history

Response to the paper on the *Masque of Anarchy*, Corbyn at Glastonbury, and the poem's recirculation.

Related: [[Peterloo and The Mask of Anarchy]] · [[Anabelle Shelley Adorno]] · [[Samuel Shelley Larkin]] · [[The Mask of Anarchy — Student Handout]]

**Verdict.** Strong paper with its own thesis buried in a subordinate clause. The job is to tell him what he already has, replace one strained move with a demonstrable one, and give him the reception fact that turns the frame into an argument.

---

## 1. Lead with this: the thesis he already wrote and passed over

Stanza 74:

> 'Be your strong and simple words
> Keen to wound as sharpened swords,
> And wide as targes let them be,
> With their shade to cover ye.

He calls *words/swords* a "visual pun." **It is an eye-rhyme, and the term matters.** The pair looks like a perfect rhyme, differing by a single initial letter, and does not rhyme in the mouth, the *w* in *sword* having been silent for centuries.

So the poem's central proposition, that words can act as weapons, is staged in a rhyme that functions only on the page, inside a poem in ballad measure written for oral delivery to an audience substantially unable to read. The poem asserts that speech can wound and simultaneously demonstrates that the assertion holds only in silent reading.

That is his whole paragraph about the poem "neither advocating revolutionary violence nor not advocating" it, delivered at the level of technique rather than theme. **Tell him: this is your argument.**

## 2. Replace the anacoluthon with something stronger

The anacoluthon reading is doing real work and does not need to be true. Stanza 89 supplies the antecedent:

> 'And that slaughter to the Nation
> Shall steam up like inspiration,
> Eloquent, oracular;
> A volcano heard afar.

The massacre becomes speech; "these words" in st. 90 are the words the slaughter has become; "shall then become / Like Oppression's thundered doom" completes cleanly. The grain already gives him what he wants.

What to use instead, and it is a fact rather than a reading:

> [!important] The refrain at stanza 91 is a repetition. It already appeared at stanza 38.
> "Heard again—again—again—" cross-references fifty-three stanzas earlier, and the next stanza performs the prophecy it has just made. The poem predicts its own repetition and then repeats.
>
> Better: in the standard text the two printings differ in pointing. St. 38 gives "In unvanquishable number," with a comma. St. 91 gives "number—" with a dash. **The refrain closes the first time and opens the second.** That is exactly the "something spills over" he intuits and cannot name, and it is sitting in the punctuation.

## 3. The reception fact the paper needs

Glastonbury is a good hook and weak evidence: it shows attenuation without showing mechanism. The mechanism is this.

**"For the many, not the few" entered the Labour Party's constitution in Tony Blair's revised Clause IV in 1995,** as part of abandoning the commitment to public ownership. It then became the title of Corbyn's 2017 manifesto and the phrase on the placards. Same six words, same line of Shelley, serving New Labour's retreat from socialism and then its attempted recovery, twenty-two years apart. Some accounts propose Pericles' Funeral Oration as a rival source; he should acknowledge that and then either set it aside or exploit it.

That is the real opening. A refrain detachable enough to serve opposed projects is exactly the property he is trying to explain, and §1 above explains it. Corbyn's slide from Shelley's "Ye" to his own "those" and "us," which Sean already catches via the ticket price, then reads as a symptom rather than an anecdote.

Small note: Corbyn's gloss that Shelley "travelled extensively around Europe" euphemises exile. Shelley left England in March 1818 and died abroad in 1822. One sentence, since the paper is about attenuation.

## 4. Citations

**Add, essential.** Michael Demson, "'Let a great Assembly be': Percy Shelley's 'The Mask of Anarchy' and the Organization of Labor in New York City, 1910–30," *European Romantic Review* 22:5 (2011), 641–665. On Pauline Newman and the literature curriculum of the ILGWU's Workers' University: precisely the claim Sean asserts unsupported. Also Demson's graphic history *Masks of Anarchy*, with Summer McClinton (Verso, 2013).

Note the poem has two assembly injunctions: "Let a great Assembly be" (st. 65) and "Let a vast assembly be" (st. 73). Demson's title takes the first.

**Ask for pages: Wolfson and Reno.** I could not verify either claim. The Reno gloss looks shaky, since "Oppression's thundered doom" is the vehicle of a simile for what *words* become, so the line concerns speech turning into judgment rather than exhorting violence. If Reno says otherwise, quote him; if Sean is compressing, fix it.

**Check the Spott date.** *We Will Bury You* is a Veer Books title and the composition context is the Commons vote on the public sector pay cap, which puts it in 2017. If he means a 2023 reissue, say so.

**Soong.** The essay is "Escapist Poetry," *Chicago Review*, treating Spott alongside Marie Buck's *Unsolved Mysteries*. Soong's operative category, that contemporary poetry can be confrontational and escapist at once, serves this paper better than the passage he quoted.

**Recast Zukofsky.** "A"-9 sets Marx's value theory inside Cavalcanti's canzone. The shared move with Shelley is economic critique under severe formal constraint, not prefiguration. Relatedly, st. 41's "So that ye for them are made / Loom, and plough, and sword, and spade" describes the worker becoming the instrument, closer to reification than to alienation proper. "Song to the Men of England" is the sharper text for extraction.

## 5. The structural advice

He ends standing before the long history wondering how to untangle it. **Tell him not to untangle it.**

The paper runs two projects side by side: a reception history of a detachable refrain, and a close reading of a poem dissatisfied with its own action. They become one project the moment the argument is that **the poem's form produces its recirculability**. The refrain travels because Shelley built it to travel: repeated it, set it in ballad measure, gave it to an indeterminate Shape rather than a positioned speaker, and rhymed its central claim for the eye rather than the ear.

Then Corbyn, the ILGWU, Tahrir Square and the poll tax stop being a list of contexts and become evidence for a claim about technique.

Keep the closing question open. What it means to rise like lions again and again is the right question, and Soong's worry that the phantasy may engender the situation it escapes has an exact formal counterpart: **a refrain designed for repetition is also a refrain that never has to be acted on once.**

## 6. Sharpen the McKay comparison

Drop proximity to violence as the axis. "If We Must Die" is a sonnet, published in *The Liberator*, July 1919, during the Red Summer. **McKay raises the register where Shelley lowers it**, taking the high form for a poem about defending against lynching while Shelley takes the popular form for a poem about defending against cavalry. That inversion is the comparison.

If he pursues it: warn him off the widely repeated claim that Churchill quoted the poem. Never substantiated.

Angelou's "Still I Rise" (in *And Still I Rise*, 1978) is well used — the individualisation of the exhortation is the right observation — but the formal contrast is available there too and he does not take it.

## Lead for me, not for him

St. 89 has the massacre "steam up like inspiration," in a poem about Manchester in 1819, with *inspiration* carrying its etymological sense of breathing in. The nation inhales what the mills exhale. "Steam up" is ordinary enough usage that this may be nothing, and I am marking it speculative, but it sits oddly well against [[Malm — Fossil Capital and Manchester]].
