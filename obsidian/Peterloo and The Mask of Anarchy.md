## The controlling absence

Andreas Malm, *Fossil Capital* (Verso, 2016), never mentions Peterloo. No St Peter's Field, no Henry Hunt, no Manchester and Salford Yeomanry, no 16 August 1819. He also names no Romantic poet: no Shelley, Byron, Blake, Wordsworth, Coleridge, Keats, Carlyle. He does have a sustained argument about verse, built on Mike Sanders, *The Poetry of Chartism* (Cambridge, 2009), and on the poetry columns of the *Northern Star*, which reprinted Shelley more than any other Romantic and whose readers called *Queen Mab* the Chartists' Bible.

Malm's chronology effectively begins with the repeal of the Combination Laws in 1824. The Shelley absence and the Peterloo absence are one absence.

## Open threads

- **Was Birley's mill steam-powered?** Hugh Hornby Birley commanded the yeomanry at Peterloo and began building a cotton mill on Cambridge Street, Chorlton-on-Medlock, in 1814. An urban Manchester mill on that site had no fall of water available and by Malm's own criterion would have run on steam. Unverified from primary sources. Check the Birley papers or the Manchester rate books before using it in print. If it holds, the man who led the sabre charge was a steam capitalist, and Malm's book connecting steam to class power has neither the poem nor the mill-owner.
- **Shelley's melting poles.** *Queen Mab* VIII (1813) imagines the poles unloosed and the deserts blooming as figures for emancipation. Shelley's image of freedom is Malm's image of catastrophe, same physical event, opposite moral sign, six years before Peterloo. The eighteenth-century commonplace that cultivation ameliorates climate stands behind this, and Shelley's notes to *Queen Mab* draw on Erasmus Darwin. Genealogy not yet traced.
- **16 August 1842.** The National Charter Association executive endorsed the general strike in Manchester on the twenty-third anniversary of Peterloo. Malm reports the date as evidence of the leadership's belatedness. Read against the anniversary, the delegates chose their day.

## Verified quotations kept here

Shelley, *A Defence of Poetry* (written 1821, published 1840):

> The cultivation of those sciences which have enlarged the limits of the empire of man over the external world, has, for want of the poetical faculty, proportionally circumscribed those of the internal world; and man, having enslaved the elements, remains himself a slave. To what but a cultivation of the mechanical arts in a degree disproportioned to the presence of the creative faculty, which is the basis of all knowledge, is to be attributed the abuse of all invention for abridging and combining labour, to the exasperation of the inequality of mankind? From what other cause has it arisen that the discoveries which should have lightened, have added a weight to the curse imposed on Adam?

Shelley, *Queen Mab* VIII (1813):

> The habitable earth is full of bliss;
> Those wastes of frozen billows that were hurled
> By everlasting snow-storms round the poles,
> Where matter dared not vegetate nor live,
> But ceaseless frost round the vast solitude
> Bound its broad zone of stillness, are unloosed;

Shelley, "Song to the Men of England" (written 1819, published 1839):

> The seed ye sow, another reaps;
> The wealth ye find, another keeps;
> The robes ye weave, another wears;
> The arms ye forge; another bears.

(Hutchinson prints a semicolon in the fourth line; most editions give a comma.)
