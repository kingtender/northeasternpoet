# Millicent · Shelley · Wordsworth

Response to the paper on verticality, "rise," and the tricolon.

Related: [[Peterloo and The Mask of Anarchy]] · [[Siqi Shelley]] · [[Nell Shelley Casanova]] · [[Sean Shelley reception history]] · [[Anabelle Shelley Adorno]]

**Verdict.** The figures she is tracking are real and the system behind them is more complete than she suspects. Best citation practice of the four papers, actual page numbers. Worst prose of the four, and it is hiding her own argument from her. Say that once, plainly, because she is plainly capable.

---

## 1. Say the hard thing first, once

"Romantic and revolutionary ethe." "The Casanova," "via the Casanova," "in the Casanova too." "Some mettle of direction." "Is up forwards?, etc."

**The "etc." is the tell.** It marks where she stopped thinking and hoped the gesture would carry. Also "in very literal special terms" is presumably *spatial*. Her figures are real; her sentences are concealing them.

## 2. Two citations to fix

- **Blanchot.** The book is ***The Writing of the Disaster*** (*L'Écriture du désastre*, 1980), not "Writing as Disaster." Ann Smock's translation is University of Nebraska Press, 1986, with a 1995 paperback. Ask where 2015 came from.
- **Wordsworth.** Cite the poem: "To Toussaint L'Ouverture," first printed in *The Morning Post*, 2 February 1803, signed W.L.D., collected in *Poems, in Two Volumes* (1807), where lines 7–9 differ from the newspaper text. Also worth noting she uses the modern *Louverture* while Wordsworth's title uses *L'Ouverture*; distinguish them once rather than sliding.

## 3. The tricolon finding, which answers her own question

She asks whether the tricolons "roll or stack" and "what kind of growth do they represent, if growth?" Count them.

**Every tricolon in the poem's first third belongs to illegitimate authority:**

| Stanza | | Speaker |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | "I AM GOD, AND KING, AND LAW!" | on Anarchy's brow |
| 15 | "Thou art God, and Law, and King." | the hired murderers |
| 16 | "Give us glory, and blood, and gold." | the murderers |
| 17 | "Thou art Law and God." | lawyers and priests (drops to two) |
| 18 | "Thou art King, and God, and Lord" | all of them together |
| 20 | "His the sceptre, crown, and globe" | narrator, on Anarchy |
| 25 | "Murder, Fraud, and Anarchy" | narrator |

**Three of these are the same triad shuffled.** God / King / Law, then God / Law / King, then King / God / Lord. The terms are interchangeable because they name one power three times: **throne, altar and bench**, the three institutions the reform movement actually opposed.

And Shelley signposts the register himself. St. 17 calls it "Like a bad prayer not over loud." St. 18 finishes "Anarchy, to thee we bow, / Be thy name made holy now!" **That is the Lord's Prayer.** The tricolon here is a liturgical parody, a blasphemous trinity, and Shelley labels it as one.

> [!important] The answer to her question
> It **stacks**, and stacking is what usurped authority does. It represents **no growth at all**, because a trinity is closed by definition, which is exactly why the shuffling can be arbitrary.

### The counter-form she missed

Liberty gets no tricolon. Liberty gets an **unbounded anaphoric series**, sts. 52–65: "For the labourer thou art bread," "Thou art clothes, and fire, and food," "To the rich thou art a check," "Thou art Justice," "Thou art Wisdom," "Thou art Peace," "Thou art Love," closing "Spirit, Patience, Gentleness, / All that can adorn and bless / Art thou."

**A closed triad for Anarchy, an open list for Liberty.** That is the formal opposition the paper is reaching for, sitting in plain view.

### The tetracolon

Occurs exactly twice, and both times names something done to bodies:

- "Loom, and plough, and sword, and spade" (st. 41) — workers turned into instruments
- "Slash, and stab, and maim, and hew,—" (st. 84) — bodies cut

**Four is the number of violence.** Her observation that Anarchy chews hearts "one by one, and two by two" and not thrice now belongs to a system rather than to a hunch.

## 4. "Is up forwards?" — no

St. 68:

> 'From the workhouse and the prison
> Where pale as corpses newly risen,
> Women, children, young and old
> Groan for pain, and weep for cold—

The poem's literal resurrection is here and it is **pallid**. The assembly is summoned out of institutions of confinement, and the people arriving have already risen, and rising has left them cold and groaning.

So the resurrection she is tracking through Blanchot has already happened before the refrain ever calls for it, and it looks like death rather than triumph. Which complicates "Rise like Lions" considerably: the poem asks for a rising from people it has just described as corpses who have already got up.

Full inventory of the rise, for her use: "A mist, a light, an image rose" (26) · "It grew—a Shape arrayed in mail" (28) · "upborne on wings" (28) · "These words of joy and fear arose" (34) · "Rise like Lions" (38) · "pale as corpses newly risen" (68) · "Shall steam up like inspiration" (89) · "Rise like Lions" (91).

## 5. Her Wordsworth link is much better than she knows

The Toussaint sonnet closes:

> Thou hast left behind
> Powers that will work for thee; air, earth, and skies;
> There's not a breathing of the common wind
> That will forget thee; thou hast great allies;
> Thy friends are exultations, agonies,
> And love, and man's unconquerable mind.

Two things.

**First, the form matches.** "Air, earth, and skies" is a tricolon; "exultations, agonies, / And love, and man's unconquerable mind" is a tetracolon. Wordsworth's sonnet runs on the same architecture, so she can make the formal comparison rather than the thematic one she currently has.

**Second, and this is the real genealogy.** Wordsworth in 1803 consoles a fallen revolutionary by promising him the elements as allies. Shelley in 1819 has the element actually speak: the "indignant Earth" turning every drop of blood "To an accent unwithstood" (sts. 35–36). **Wordsworth offers the earth as a friend; Shelley gives it the microphone.** That is about the elements as political agents, not about verticality, and it is a much stronger thread.

**The sting.** Wordsworth addresses a singular *thou*, alone in a dungeon, and offers comfort. Shelley addresses a plural *ye* and offers none. Wordsworth consoles the isolated man; Shelley refuses consolation and convenes an assembly. Her "rise" thread has been circling that difference without naming it. Cross-reference [[Siqi Shelley]] §7: in the *Masque* the singular is the grammatical form of destitution, so Wordsworth's whole consolatory gesture is addressed to the position Shelley identifies as slavery.

## 6. Two loose ends to make her close

**"Is it symbol?"** She asks this in the first paragraph and never returns. It is the paper's real question and she abandoned it in the opening. Send her back to it.

**The Casanova move is a pun.** Her "rising above" into classic status (p. 92) and Shelley's people rising off the ground are the same word doing unrelated work. She should say so out loud and then argue that the pun is productive, which it may well be: a poem whose content is a literal call to rise achieved exactly the Casanovan escape from being "condemned to be dated," and achieved it through the one line that says so. Naming the pun as a pun costs nothing and buys the argument its integrity.
