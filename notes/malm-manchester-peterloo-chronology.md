# Manchester in Two Chronologies

## Andreas Malm, *Fossil Capital*, against the run-up to Peterloo

Working notes. Compiled 4 September 2026.

---

### Method and warnings

The left column contains only what Malm puts on the page in *Fossil Capital: The Rise of Steam Power and the Roots of Global Warming* (Verso, 2016). Chapter numbers refer to the epub; the edition carries no page numbers in that format, so citation is by chapter. Every quotation is transcribed exactly as it appears in the text, including its British spelling and its `[sic]` marks where Malm supplies them.

The right column contains the political sequence Malm omits. Those entries are drawn from the standard scholarship and from institutional sources, listed at the end. Where the record varies, the variance is stated rather than smoothed.

Three claims in these notes are inference and are marked as such in place. Everything else is either quotation from Malm or established fact.

The controlling absence: **Malm never mentions Peterloo.** The word does not appear in the body, the notes, or the index. Neither does St Peter's Field, Henry Hunt, the Manchester and Salford Yeomanry, or the date 16 August 1819. The year 1819 appears twice in the book and means something else both times.

### Where the seam opens

Four problems, sorted by kind.

**(c) A counter-argument in the literature he does not engage.** Malm's periodization is a deliberate polemic against the standard 1780–1830 frame. He wants the transition to steam dated to 1825–1848 and explained by the spatial politics of labour rather than by coal's cheapness or Malthusian scarcity. That is the argument's strength and it is also why everything before 1824 becomes prologue. E. P. Thompson is in the apparatus, cited three times from *The Making of the English Working Class* (New York, 1966): at p. 282 and ch. 9 for the weavers, at p. 520 for the Combination Laws. Dorothy Thompson supplies the Chartist material. The Manchester radical formation of 1816 to 1819 falls into the seam between the two, cited by neither citation.

**(b) An assumption that needs examination.** Malm's mechanism is that steam let capital treat workers as "so many old shuttles," Walter Scott's phrase, because the town supplied an anonymous, replaceable, already-disciplined mass. He dates the strategic value of that substitutability to after 1825, on the ground that repeal of the Combination Laws "restricted the options of physical attacks on unions and strikers." The formulation assumes that before 1824 the physical option was available and adequate. Peterloo is the strongest evidence for that assumption and its most serious complication at once, because the massacre demonstrated that the militarized option was politically unsustainable, which is plausibly part of why repeal arrived five years later. Malm inherits the consequence without narrating the cause, and the assumption sits load-bearing and unexamined.

**(d) An implication he does not draw.** Malm's own Chapter 6 records that the *Manchester Guardian* was founded in 1821 by a cotton dealer. That is a fact about Peterloo. Taylor witnessed the massacre and worked on the relief fund, and the paper was founded in its aftermath as the organ of a reforming commercial class that needed to distance itself from the yeomanry without conceding anything to the radicals. Malm then uses the *Guardian* as the unmediated voice of the cotton bourgeoisie for two hundred pages. That the bourgeoisie's own paper was born out of a massacre it had to disown is precisely the institutional genealogy his method is built to trace, and he leaves it untouched.

**(a) A logical gap that needs evidence, and the sharpest one.** Hugh Hornby Birley, who commanded the Manchester and Salford Yeomanry at Peterloo and is generally held to have led the charge, was a Manchester cotton manufacturer whose Cambridge Street mill, begun 1814, stood under a mile from St Peter's Field and had been picketed by the spinners in 1818. *Inference, flagged:* an urban Manchester mill of that scale on Cambridge Street had no fall of water available and by Malm's own criterion would have run on steam. I have not verified the prime mover from a primary source, and the claim should be checked in the Birley papers or in the Manchester rate books before it is used in print. If it holds, the man who put the sabre into the crowd was a steam-mill owner defending a mill the crowd's own trade had struck the previous summer, which converts Malm's argument from a story about the spatial logic of energy into a story about armed force, and dates the joining of steam power to state violence six years before the crash on which his whole chronology turns.

---

### Two further inferences, flagged

*My inference, not Malm's:* the NCA executive endorsed the general strike in Manchester on 16 August 1842, the anniversary of Peterloo. Malm gives the date and reads it as belatedness. Read against the anniversary, the delegates chose their day, and the endorsement is a citation rather than a lag.

*My inference:* the Blanketeers of 10 March 1817 and the crowd of 16 August 1819 assembled on the same ground, and the difference between the two outcomes is the difference between regular cavalry and a yeomanry troop of local manufacturers policing their own workforce. Malm's category of the urban steam capitalist, free to "assemble five hundred workmen one week and dismiss them the next," has an armed form he never names.
